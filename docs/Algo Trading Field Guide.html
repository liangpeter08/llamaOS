Algo Trading Field Guide

Every major idea in event-driven, LLM-assisted intraday trading: what it means, why it matters, what usually goes wrong, and where to read more. Enough to talk like an expert; links for when you need to become one.

00

The short answer

The premise is that Claude should find short-lived, event-driven edge. Half of that holds up.

Won't work

Being fast on news

Professional firms react to a headline in microseconds. Your path runs through a news vendor, Alpaca, your server and an LLM call that takes seconds. The obvious reading of a headline is priced in before you act, and an LLM makes you slower.

Can work

Reading news better

Prices often under-react to news that is complex, ambiguous, about small firms, or has second-order effects. The drift can last minutes to days. LLMs read that kind of text at scale, and papers find real predictive power, mostly in smaller stocks and shrinking over time.

What to expect. If you find an edge it will be small (a few basis points per trade after costs), noisy (many losing days) and decaying (others will find it too). The most likely outcome of a first system is a great backtest and a losing live record. Most of this guide is about telling the two apart before you pay for the lesson.

Retail day traders lose money on average; large studies in Taiwan and Brazil find that most persistent day traders lose. Automation removes emotional errors but not the economics. The realistic advantage for a small operator is capacity: strategies too small for a fund to bother with.

If you can't say who is on the other side of your trade and why they are willing to lose to you, you have a pattern, not an edge.

Go deeper

  • PaperGrossman & Stiglitz (1980), On the Impossibility of Informationally Efficient Markets. Why some edge must exist for anyone to bother gathering information.
  • PaperChague, De-Losso & Giovannetti (2019), Day Trading for a Living?
  • PaperBarber, Lee, Liu & Odean (2014), The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill: Evidence from Day Trading.
  • BookErnest Chan, Quantitative Trading (2nd ed., 2021), ch. 1–2. A candid view of what a small trader can do.

The critical challenges

Ranked roughly from most to least common. These are what actually kill strategies.

fatal		Backtest Sharpe 3, live Sharpe −0.5You and Claude tried many ideas. The best one is best partly by luck, and the more you search, the more luck you find.
2Look-ahead bias
fatal		The backtest knows things it couldn't have knownTimestamps, revised data, survivorship, and an LLM that already read about what happened after the event.

fatal		Fills systematically at worse pricesAt news time the people filling you know more or are faster. A fast fill often means you were wrong.

serious		"It won 14 of 20 trades"Event strategies produce few trades. Twenty trades say almost nothing statistically.

02

Foundations: what edge means

Alpha and beta
Beta is the part of your return explained by the market moving. Alpha is what remains after removing market and known-factor exposure. A strategy that is always long good news in a bull market may be mostly beta.
Expected value per trade
EV = P(win) × avg_win − P(loss) × avg_loss − costs. Win rate alone means nothing: 70% winners with small wins and large losses can still lose.
Sharpe ratio
Mean excess return divided by its standard deviation, annualised (×√252 for daily). Under 0.5 is weak, about 1 is good, above 2 is rare for a real, scalable strategy. A backtest above 3 is more often a bug or overfit than a discovery.
Sortino, Calmar, drawdown
Variants that penalise downside or the worst peak-to-trough loss. How long you stay underwater matters as much as how deep.
Fundamental Law
IR ≈ IC × √breadth. IC is the correlation between forecast and outcome (0.02–0.05 is typical and good); breadth is the number of independent bets. Each news signal is weak, so you need many of them.
Efficient markets
Prices reflect public information quickly, especially in large stocks. Inefficiencies live at the edges: small caps, complex information, and places that are hard to arbitrage.
Limits to arbitrage
Mispricings persist because exploiting them is costly or risky (short constraints, capital limits, noise-trader risk). An edge that still exists is usually hard to exploit.
Factors
Systematic return drivers: market, size, value, momentum, quality, low volatility, short-term reversal. Buying small caps after news may just be size plus momentum exposure.
Alpha decay
How fast a signal's predictive power fades after the event. It sets your holding period.
Capacity
How much capital a strategy can trade before its own costs eat the edge. Small capacity works in your favour because funds won't compete for it.

Go deeper

03

Market microstructure

You can't model costs or news reactions without knowing how trades actually happen.

Limit order book
Resting bids and asks at each price. The best bid and best ask form the top of book; the gap between them is the bid-ask spread.
Market vs limit orders
Market orders take liquidity and pay the spread. Limit orders provide liquidity, may not fill, and tend to fill exactly when you'd rather they didn't.
Market impact
Your own order moving the price. It grows roughly with the square root of your size relative to daily volume (the square-root law).
Adverse selection
Market makers widen spreads when they suspect informed flow. At news time spreads widen sharply, so trading costs peak exactly when you want to trade.
US market structure
About 16 lit exchanges plus dark pools and wholesalers. Reg NMS routes to the best displayed price (the NBBO). Retail orders are often filled by wholesalers paid through payment for order flow.
SIP vs direct feeds
The SIP is the consolidated public feed; HFTs use faster direct feeds. Alpaca's free data tier is IEX only, one exchange with a small share of volume, so prices and volumes can differ from the full market. Use SIP-quality data for research and cost modelling.
Sessions and auctions
Pre-market 4:00–9:30 ET, regular 9:30–16:00, after-hours 16:00–20:00. Earnings usually land outside regular hours, when liquidity is thin. Opening and closing auctions are their own liquidity events.
Halts and LULD
Limit Up-Limit Down bands pause a stock that moves too fast, and news-pending halts are common around big announcements. Your system must handle "can't trade right now".
Gaps
Price jumps across sessions or halts. A stop at $10 fills at the first available trade, which might be $8.
Short selling
Needs a borrow (locate); hard-to-borrow names carry fees. After a 10% drop, Rule 201 (SSR) restricts shorting to upticks.
HFT and market making
Speed-based strategies earning the spread and latency arbitrage. You won't compete with them, but they are the counterparty to many of your fills.

04

Event-driven and news trading

This is your core discipline.

Event study
Align many events at t = 0, measure abnormal returns (return minus expected return, such as the market or sector), and average them into the cumulative abnormal return (CAR). Idea Lab already does this; learn the method properly, because everything else builds on it.
Event types
Different events behave differently.
  • Scheduled: earnings, guidance, FDA dates, CPI, FOMC. The market knows when, so only the surprise matters.
  • Unscheduled: M&A, lawsuits, CEO exits, contracts, short reports, rating changes. Bigger surprise, noisier, often halted.
  • Macro: moves everything at once, so diversified news bets become one bet.
Surprise vs expectation
"Revenue up 20%" means nothing without consensus. A beat smaller than the whisper number can fall. Standardised unexpected earnings (SUE) is the classic measure.
Under-reaction and drift
The best-documented news anomaly. Post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD): prices keep moving in the surprise direction for days to weeks. Similar drift follows other news, especially in small, lightly covered stocks and for soft or complex information.
Over-reaction and reversal
Big moves on no identifiable news tend to reverse; moves on real news tend to continue (Chan 2003). Attention-grabbing, retail-heavy moves often overshoot.
Stale news
Reprinted or old news sometimes still moves prices, then reverses. Your pipeline must ask whether an item is actually new.
Sentiment vs information
Tone is not the same as fundamental information. Dictionary sentiment (Loughran-McDonald) was the pre-LLM standard; LLMs read meaning, not only tone.
Attention and coverage
Fewer analysts and smaller caps mean slower reactions: more edge, but higher costs and less capacity.
Which clock
Publish time, vendor receive time or your receive time? A few seconds of mis-timing can create fake edge in a backtest.
Relevance and entity linking
Is the story about this company, or does it only mention it? Professional feeds score relevance and novelty; you have to build that yourself.
Spillover effects
News about one firm moves suppliers, customers and competitors, and these links predict returns. This suits LLM reasoning and is less crowded than direct reactions.

05

LLMs in trading

What the evidence says

LLMs can extract signal from news. Lopez-Lira & Tang (2023) found that ChatGPT's reading of headlines predicted next-day returns, more strongly in small caps and for bad news, and that the effect declined as LLM use spread.

Text-based prediction worked before LLMs. Ke, Kelly & Xiu (2019) did it with simpler models. LLMs are a better tool for a known idea.

Most published LLM backtests are contaminated. The model was trained on text written after the events it "predicts", so it may simply remember what happened. Glasserman & Lin (2023) show the effect is real. This is the main LLM-specific pitfall.

Key concepts

Training contamination
Events before the model's training cutoff can leak their outcome. Trust LLM backtests only on events after the cutoff, or on anonymised text. Forward paper trading is the cleanest test.
Anonymisation test
Replace "Apple" with "Company X" and strip dates and identifying numbers. If performance collapses, the model was remembering rather than reasoning.
Point-in-time models
Researchers now train "chronologically consistent" LLMs on data up to a fixed date for exactly this reason. Record which model produced every historical signal.
Version drift
Same prompt, different answers; model updates change behaviour. Log model ID, prompt version and output for every decision. Treat a model upgrade as a new strategy that needs re-validation.
Latency budget
A large-model call takes seconds. Use tiered triage (cheap filter, then a deep read only when it matters), as llamaOS does, and accept that you trade the drift rather than the first tick.
Calibration
"Confidence 0.8" is not a probability. Check how often the model was right each time it said 0.8, using a reliability plot.
Features, not decisions
The safer pattern, and yours: the LLM produces structured features (event type, direction, magnitude, novelty, confidence) and deterministic, testable rules place the trade. This keeps the system auditable and backtestable.
Hypothesis generator
An LLM proposing ideas is a multiple-testing machine. Test 200 hypotheses and about 10 look significant at p < 0.05 by chance. Count every one (Idea Lab's hypothesis counter) and correct for it (section 07).
Prompt injection
News text is untrusted input; a crafted press release could try to steer an LLM trader. Constrain outputs to a schema and keep the risk guard deterministic.
Cost
API cost per item times thousands of items a day is a transaction cost. It belongs in your P&L.

Backtesting and research method

A backtest simulates what would have happened. Every shortcut in it is optimistic.

The biases to memorise

BiasWhat it isDefence
Look-aheadUsing information not available at decision time: the close for a 10:00 trade, revised earnings, future news timestampsPoint-in-time data; decide at t, fill at t+1. llamaOS fills at next-bar open, which is right.
SurvivorshipTesting only on stocks that still existA point-in-time universe including delisted tickers
Data snoopingReporting the best of many triesCount trials; deflate the Sharpe; keep an untouched holdout
OverfittingToo many parameters tuned to historyFewer parameters; check that small parameter changes don't break it
Fill optimismFills at mid, at your limit, with no impactModel spread, slippage and partial fills, worse at news time
Warm-up errorsIndicators using future data or not warmed upUnit tests on feature timestamps
Corporate actionsSplits and dividends unadjusted, or adjusted with future infoProperly adjusted point-in-time data
Clock errorsUTC vs ET, DST, vendor vs publish timeOne canonical clock; test DST boundaries
Narrative biasBelieving it because the story is goodWrite down in advance what result would make you drop it
LLM contaminationThe model knows the future (section 05)Post-cutoff tests, anonymisation, forward paper trading

Research process

In-sample, out-of-sample, holdout
Develop on one period, test on another, and keep a final holdout you look at once. Every peek turns it into in-sample data.
Walk-forward
Repeatedly fit on a rolling past window and test on the next one. Closer to how you will actually trade.
Purged CV
Ordinary k-fold leaks information in time series. Purged and embargoed cross-validation removes training samples whose labels overlap the test period.
Triple-barrier labels
Label each trade by which comes first: take-profit, stop-loss or time limit. It matches how bracket orders actually exit.
Meta-labelling
A primary model picks the side; a secondary model decides whether to take the trade and how big. A natural fit for "LLM proposes, filter decides".
Robustness checks
Shift entry by one bar, move thresholds by 10%, drop the best five trades, run a different year. Fragile results are usually fitted noise.
Paper trading
Forward test for a fixed, pre-agreed period. Immune to look-ahead and snooping, though paper fills are still optimistic.
Live reconciliation
Compare each live trade with what the backtester would have done on the same data. The gap is your real simulation error.

Go deeper

  • BookMarcos López de Prado, Advances in Financial Machine Learning (2018). Purged CV, triple barrier, meta-labelling. Dense and opinionated, but essential.
  • PaperBailey et al. (2017), The Probability of Backtest Overfitting.
  • PaperHarvey & Liu (2015), Backtesting.

Statistics for not fooling yourself

You don't need a statistics degree. You need these ideas.

Signal-to-noise
A real edge might shift the mean by 0.05% while the daily standard deviation is 2%. You need many observations to see it.
How many trades
Roughly n ≈ (2 / SR_per_trade)² independent trades to be confident the Sharpe is above zero. A per-trade Sharpe of 0.1 (good) needs about 400 trades. A few dozen prove nothing.
t-statistic
t ≈ mean / (std / √n). After multiple testing, t > 2 is too low a bar; Harvey, Liu & Zhu argue for t > 3.
Multiple testing
Bonferroni (strict), Holm, Benjamini-Hochberg (false discovery rate). Test 100 junk ideas and expect about 5 "significant" ones.
Deflated Sharpe
Adjusts a backtest Sharpe for the number of strategies tried, their variance and non-normal returns. Apply it to Idea Lab output.
Reality Check / SPA
White's and Hansen's formal tests of whether the best strategy beats chance, given everything you tried.
Permutation tests
Shuffle event dates or signs and re-run. If random events give similar results, the edge is fake. Very practical for event studies.
Fat tails and skew
Returns aren't normal. Strategies that win small and often but lose big occasionally (fading spikes, selling volatility) look great until they don't.
Independence
Overlapping holding periods and many news items on one day are not independent. Count independent bets, not trades.
Base rates
Before claiming 60% directional accuracy, check what always guessing "up" scores.
Regression to the mean
Your best month is usually followed by a more ordinary one. Don't scale up after a hot streak.

08

Transaction costs and execution

For short-horizon strategies, costs decide whether you make money. Gross edges of a few basis points are common, and so are costs of a few basis points.

Basis point
0.01%. Think about everything per trade in bps.
Explicit costs
Commissions (zero for stocks at Alpaca), small SEC/FINRA fees on sells, borrow fees, data fees and LLM API fees.
Implicit costs
  • Spread: half the spread each way. Large caps 1–3 bps, small caps 20–100+ bps, much wider at news time.
  • Slippage: decision price vs fill price.
  • Impact: your order moving the price; matters in small caps.
  • Opportunity cost: limit orders that missed the winners, leaving you filled mostly on losers.
Implementation shortfall
Decision price vs actual average fill, including the unfilled part. The right cost metric to track live.
Order types
Market, limit, marketable limit, stop, stop-limit, bracket (entry plus take-profit and stop), OCO, and time-in-force (DAY, IOC, GTC). Know how each behaves in a gap or halt.
Execution algorithms
TWAP, VWAP, POV and Almgren-Chriss. Mostly for larger sizes, but the trade-off between impact and waiting applies to everyone.
Cost model
At minimum half_spread + slippage(vol, size/ADV) + fees, made worse at news time and outside regular hours. If it only works with zero costs, it doesn't work.
Breakeven cost
The per-trade cost at which the strategy breaks even. If that is below a realistic cost, stop.

Risk management and sizing

Edge decides whether you make money. Sizing decides whether you survive long enough to find out.

Risk per trade
Size so a stop-out loses a fixed small fraction of capital, often 0.25–1%. Gaps can make the real loss larger.
Volatility targeting
Size inversely to volatility (for example ATR-based) so each trade carries similar risk.
Kelly criterion
The growth-optimal bet size given your edge and odds. Edge estimates are always overstated, so use a fraction: a quarter to a half, often less.
Risk of ruin
The chance of losing enough that you can't continue. Driven by sizing and losing streaks more than average return.
Correlation
Five news trades in one sector, or on a macro day, are one bet. Cap sector, theme, gross and net exposure.
Drawdown controls
Daily loss limit, max-drawdown kill switch, cool-down periods. risk.yaml and the kill switch are the right shape.
Stop-losses
They cap normal losses, fill badly in gaps, and tight intraday stops get hit by noise. Time-based exits often work as well for event strategies.
Tail risk
Ask: what's my loss if the stock opens down 50% and I can't exit for an hour?
VaR and expected shortfall
The loss you exceed X% of the time, and the average loss when you do. Useful for reporting, unreliable under fat tails.
Retirement rule
Decide in advance when to retire a strategy whose live results fall outside its backtest range.

Operations and failure modes

A correct strategy can still lose everything through engineering failures. Professionals spend more effort here than on alpha.

Separation of concerns
Signals, then portfolio and risk, then execution, then reconciliation. llamaOS mirrors this: models propose, deterministic code trades.
Pre-trade checks
Max order size, max position, price collar, orders per minute, symbol lists, buying power. Run them in the order path, not as a report afterwards.
Kill switch
One action that cancels all orders and flattens or freezes. Test it regularly.
Reconciliation
Internal positions must match the broker's. Missed fills, partial fills and restarts cause many real losses.
Idempotency
Retrying a failed request must not duplicate an order. Use client order IDs.
Restarts
After a crash with open orders, rebuild state from the broker, not from memory.
Calendar and clock
Holidays, half-days, DST, early closes, NTP-synced clocks.
Stale data
Missing or stale data should stop trading, not trigger it. Detect staleness actively.
Deployment
Knight Capital reactivated old code on one server. Deploy outside market hours, use flags carefully, keep a rollback.
Audit trail
Every signal, model input and output, decision, order and fill, timestamped. You need it to prove whether an edge was real.
Monitoring
P&L vs expected, fill rates, slippage, error rates, latency, model failures.
Security
Keys stored safely, 2FA, least-privilege keys, no live keys on dev machines.

Rules, accounts and tax (US retail)

Not legal or tax advice. Some of these rules are changing, so check current versions.

Pattern Day Trader
FINRA 4210: four or more day trades in five business days in a margin account has required $25,000 minimum equity. FINRA has proposed replacing this with a different intraday margin framework; check the current status before planning around it.
Settlement (T+1)
US equities settle one business day later. In a cash account, trading with unsettled proceeds can cause good-faith violations.
Margin
Reg T allows 2× overnight; PDT accounts get 4× intraday. Leverage multiplies edge and ruin alike.
Short rules
Reg SHO locate requirement and Rule 201 (SSR).
Manipulation
Spoofing, layering, wash trading and pump-and-dump are illegal whether or not a bot did it. Trading on social-media hype can drift toward this. Never trade on material non-public information.
Taxes
Short-term gains are ordinary income. The wash sale rule disallows losses if you rebuy within 30 days, which day trading does constantly. Trader Tax Status and the Section 475(f) mark-to-market election can change this; ask a tax professional.
Broker terms
API rate limits, data redistribution limits, and what Alpaca's paper environment doesn't simulate. Paper fills are more optimistic than real ones.

  • DocsIRS, Topic 429: Traders in Securities.
  • DocsIRS, Publication 550 (wash sales).

Learning path

About 10–12 weeks part-time. Each stage pairs reading with a task in llamaOS and a check you should be able to pass.

  1. Mental modelweeks 1–2

    ReadSections 00–02; Chan ch. 1–3; Narang ch. 1–5
    DoFor each strategy, write down who is on the other side and why they'd lose
    CheckCan you explain alpha vs beta and the Fundamental Law in two sentences?

  2. How markets workweeks 3–4

    ReadSections 03 and 08; selected chapters of Harris
    DoMeasure spreads and slippage on paper fills at news times vs normal times; compare IEX and SIP where possible
    CheckCan you estimate the round-trip cost of your typical trade in bps?

  3. Event studiesweeks 4–6

    ReadSection 04; MacKinlay; Bernard & Thomas; Chan 2003
    DoRun Idea Lab event studies by event type × market cap × time of day; plot CAR with confidence bands
    CheckDo you know how long the drift lasts for your best event type?

  4. Not fooling yourselfweeks 6–8

    ReadSections 06–07; Bailey et al.; Harvey-Liu-Zhu; AFML ch. 7 and 11–14
    DoAdd deflated Sharpe and permutation tests to Idea Lab; run the anonymisation test; test only post-cutoff events
    CheckCan you state how many hypotheses you've tested and your deflated Sharpe?

  5. Surviveweeks 8–10

    ReadSections 09–10; Carver; the Knight Capital order
    DoReview risk.yaml limits; test the kill switch and restart reconciliation
    CheckDo you know your worst-case loss in a halted gap?
